# Woodworking cheat sheet

> **Shop safety.** Wear eye and hearing protection and a dust mask or respirator for sanding and sawing. Keep long hair and loose sleeves tied back, and don't wear gloves near rotating tools like a table saw, router table or drill press, because they can pull your hand in. Keep your hands out of the blade's path, use a push stick or block, and never cut freehand on a table saw (always use the fence or miter gauge). Unplug a tool before changing blades or bits. Never use a dull blade; it forces you to push harder.

## Lumber sizes: nominal vs. actual

| Sold as | Actual size |
|---|---|
| 1×4 | ¾" × 3½" |
| 1×6 | ¾" × 5½" |
| 2×4 | 1½" × 3½" |
| 2×6 | 1½" × 5½" |

Plan your cuts from actual dimensions. Check with a tape measure; plywood is often a bit under nominal too.

## Board feet

Board feet = thickness (in) × width (in) × length (in) ÷ 144.
Example: a 1" × 6" × 96" board = 1 × 6 × 96 ÷ 144 = 4 board feet. (For rough-sawn lumber, thickness is quoted in quarters: 4/4 = 1", 8/4 = 2".)

## Common joints

| Joint | Good for | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Butt | Quick frames | Weakest; reinforce with screws, dowels or biscuits |
| Pocket-hole | Face frames, cabinets | Fast; needs a jig; hidden screws |
| Dado / rabbet | Shelves, case sides | Strong and easy to align |
| Mortise & tenon | Table legs/rails, frames | Very strong; classic |
| Dovetail | Drawers, boxes | Strong in tension; the "show-off" joint |
| Miter | Picture frames, corners | Pretty, but weak without splines or a reinforcing key |

## Grain and glue

- Glue long grain to long grain for a strong joint; end grain soaks up glue and bonds poorly.
- Wood moves with humidity, mostly across the grain, so a wide solid panel glued rigidly into a frame can crack. Leave room for seasonal movement.
- Clamp with even pressure; wipe squeeze-out before it dries or it will block stain.

## Sanding progression

Start with the coarsest grit needed to remove marks (80–120), then step up without skipping more than one level: 80 → 120 → 180 → 220. Stop around 180–220 for most stained projects; finer can keep stain from absorbing.

## Finishing quick guide

| Finish | Character |
|---|---|
| Oil (e.g. tung, Danish) | Natural look, easy repair, less protection |
| Wipe-on poly | Durable, easy to apply |
| Shellac | Fast-drying, great sealer, not water- or heat-resistant |
| Paint | Full coverage; prime first |

Rags soaked with oil finishes can heat up and ignite on their own. Lay them flat to dry outdoors or submerge them in water in a sealed metal container before disposing.
